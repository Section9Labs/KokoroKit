# Spanish expression and conversational narration

Research note, 2026-10-20. Scope: Spanish questions, commands, exclamations, discourse timing, and a contextual evaluation set for lightweight Kokoro narration. This note changes no runtime behavior. The examples below are newly authored; no atlas prompts, recordings, or pronunciation dictionaries are reproduced. Research findings are distinguished from engineering proposals and untested performance targets.

## What the evidence supports

**An imperative does not have one obligatory Spanish melody.** Robles-Puente's production and perception experiments examine several Peninsular varieties. Declarative-like contours can convey commands; stronger imperative interpretations also involve combinations of pitch range, pitch height, and local accent configuration. Different strategies have different perceptual weights. The paper explicitly cautions that a one-word imperative cannot implement a relationship between two accents that requires a longer utterance. Therefore, a multiword command recipe cannot simply be pasted onto *¡Espera!*; the word still has lexical stress on *pe*. This study does not establish a universal duration or loudness setting for commands. [Robles-Puente 2011, full proceedings paper](https://www.lingref.com/cpp/larp/5/paper2644.pdf).

**Sp_ToBI describes observed structure, rather than functioning as an emotion API.** The training materials associate the same `L* L%` configuration with broad-focus statements, imperative wh-questions, and the end of alternative questions. Shared notation does not mean identical intentions, and different intentions need not demand different final pitch directions. The regional atlas separates information questions, confirmation, counterexpectational echoes, requests, commands, and vocatives through contextual elicitation. Its regional inventories are a useful model for evaluation categories, not a universal lookup table. [UPF Sp_ToBI training](https://sp-tobi.upf.edu/labelling_system/tonal_representation/nuclear_configurations/lslb), [Guadalajara atlas inventory](https://prosodia.upf.edu/atlasentonacion/enquestes/espanol/guadalajara/index-english.html).

**Polar and wh-questions must remain distinct, with regional and contextual qualifications.** Mendoza's Mexico City conversational data document rising nuclear configurations in information-seeking polar questions and confirmation questions, low endings in neutral wh-questions, and high endings in some biased wh-questions. Sosa's earlier comparison of four Latin American varieties finds important differences between read questions and spontaneous interview questions; a reading-list contour is not automatically a conversational norm. These findings support testing complete contexts and distinguishing question types, not making every `?` rise. They also do not justify treating all Latin American voices as one intonational variety. [Mendoza 2023](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/borealis/en/article/view/6978), [Sosa 2003](https://revistes.uab.cat/catjl/article/view/v2-sosa).

**Belief and disbelief are contextual interpretations.** Armstrong and Prieto test two Puerto Rican polar-question contours against different discourse biases. Both context and contour affect interpretation, including an ironic interpretation when they conflict. A polar contour with a low ending occurs in this variety; final rise is not a language-wide requirement. Escandell-Vidal analyzes multiple Castilian polar-question configurations and their contribution to interpretation; a syntactic polar label alone is insufficient to choose one. [Armstrong and Prieto 2015, authors' university abstract](https://www.upf.edu/en/web/traduccio/publicacions?_ppc_WAR_ppcportlet_INSTANCE_rs0mzT33f9EX_mvcRenderCommandName=%2Fppc%2Fdetail&_ppc_WAR_ppcportlet_INSTANCE_rs0mzT33f9EX_ppcId=ARE46621&_ppc_WAR_ppcportlet_INSTANCE_rs0mzT33f9EX_ppcLanguage=CAT&_ppc_WAR_ppcportlet_INSTANCE_rs0mzT33f9EX_ppcType=ARE&p_p_id=ppc_WAR_ppcportlet_INSTANCE_rs0mzT33f9EX&p_p_lifecycle=0&p_p_mode=view&p_p_state=normal), [Escandell-Vidal 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0023830917698178).

**Echoes are not ordinary questions made louder.** González and Reglero's contextual elicitation with 14 female Northern Peninsular speakers compares information-seeking, repetition, and surprise wh-in-situ questions. Broad contour shapes can resemble each other while nuclear peaks and tonal ranges differ. The study is evidence for context-sensitive local realization, not for one globally raised pitch setting or a universal rule for all speakers. [González and Reglero, published online 2023](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/intonation-correlates-of-canonical-and-noncanonical-whinsitu-questions-in-spanish/FEEF4FC9D0969F66492035FBEB33C2B1).

**Exclamation, surprise, and irony are separate dimensions.** Fliessbach's Madrid study distinguishes expectations and unexpectedness; its wh-exclamatives do not significantly differ from neutral declaratives in the examined intonational marking. Thus an exclamative need not express surprise. Becerra Valderrama and Igoa González's four experiments on Spanish irony find contextual information especially important for interpreting mockery and criticism. Rhetorical wording does not license automatic sarcastic delivery. The latter paper concerns ironic interpretation, not a universal acoustic template for rhetorical questions. [Fliessbach 2023, author institution's research summary](https://www.geisteswissenschaften.fu-berlin.de/en/we05/forschung/publications/sprachwiss/2023-fliessbach/index.html), [Becerra Valderrama and Igoa González 2013, author-uploaded paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317504061_IRONIC_INTENT_IN_HYPERBOLES_AND_RHETORICAL_QUESTIONS_IN_SPANISH).

**Narrator tags deserve their own structural treatment.** Montaño and Alías compare four narrators, one per language including Spanish, telling the same story. Post-character attribution, description, and neutral narration show differences involving pitch, intensity, pauses, and voice quality. Descriptive material has more pauses; speech-rate differences are not uniformly maintained across narrators. This small study supports keeping discourse roles distinct, but does not establish population norms or an audiobook pause table. In particular, “descriptive” does not mean “slow every word.” [Montaño and Alías 2015, ISCA paper](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/montano15_interspeech.pdf).

## Sixteen original contextual minimal pairs

These are **contextual contrasts**, not claims that each has a unique phonological contour. Each row retains the same target words in A and B; punctuation and context may change. The cues are descriptive hypotheses for native-speaker elicitation and listening review. They are not measured Kokoro results, fixed pitch curves, or mandatory acting directions. Keep the same speaker and regional variety within each comparison. Words named for prominence retain their normal lexical stress.

| ID | Context A and target | Context B and target | Contrast to listen for; lightweight boundary implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| ES-X01 | A narrator reports what the guard does outside: **«Espera.»** | A companion sees the guard step toward a broken stair: **«¡Espera!»** | Report versus urgent directive. B should sound immediately addressed, with salient stressed *pe* and decisive articulation. No universal pitch direction; do not insert a long anticipatory pause merely because B is one word. |
| ES-X02 | A witness answers what the guard is doing: **«Espera.»** | An investigator checks that same description: **«¿Espera?»** | Assertion versus confirmation of the proposition “he waits.” Preserve the question token and a complete unit; a rise is variety-dependent. Neither is automatically a command to wait. |
| ES-X03 | A companion calmly asks for another moment while finding a key: **«Espera.»** | That companion abruptly remembers a missing clue while the other person is speaking: **«¡Espera!»** | Both are directives; B also claims the floor. A may be softer or less abrupt without being slower in every syllable. Timing must come from the interruption context, not the verb alone. |
| ES-X04 | A host checks tomorrow's plans: **«¿Vienes mañana?»** | A host has just heard that the guest will cross the country overnight despite a canceled flight: **«¿Vienes mañana?»** | Information request versus incredulous confirmation. B may make *mañana* more prominent or expand the local excursion. Identical text and punctuation require different contextual evidence. |
| ES-X05 | A caretaker genuinely needs the missing object's location: **«¿Dónde lo dejaste?»** | The same caretaker has asked repeatedly and now demands an answer: **«¿Dónde lo dejaste?»** | Neutral wh-question versus insistent directive question. Expect stronger address or prominence in B; do not convert all wh-questions into rising polar questions or uniformly accelerate them. |
| ES-X06 | Noise masks the last part of an answer about a delivery: **«¿Lo mandaste dónde?»** | The answer was audible, but the stated destination is absurdly remote: **«¿Lo mandaste dónde?»** | Echo repair versus surprise echo. Both can highlight *dónde*; B may have a larger local range. Evaluate the repair/surprise distinction in context rather than demand opposite final directions. |
| ES-X07 | An organizer is genuinely collecting preferences for an unusual prize: **«¿Quién querría eso?»** | The organizer rejects an obviously unusable prize, implying nobody would want it: **«¿Quién querría eso?»** | Information request versus rhetorical rejection. A projects an answer; B may continue into an explanation without a conversational answer slot. Rhetorical does not necessarily mean angry or sarcastic. |
| ES-X08 | A host presents a choice of drinks and expects one selection: **«¿Quieres té o café?»** | A clerk only needs to know whether either of the two drinks is wanted: **«¿Quieres té o café?»** | Alternative question versus polar reading of a disjunction. A groups the alternatives contrastively; B permits a yes/no answer. The presence of *o* alone cannot establish an alternative-question melody or pause. |
| ES-X09 | A speaker remembers the time imperfectly and checks it: **«Llegas a las ocho, ¿no?»** | A supervisor reminds an employee of an already agreed time: **«Llegas a las ocho, ¿no?»** | Genuine tag confirmation versus expectation/enforcement. Preserve the relation between assertion and tag. The tag may be lightly attached or separately prominent; do not impose one upward tag contour. |
| ES-X10 | A visitor admires a carefully restored room: **«¡Qué bonito!»** | The visitor sees that someone has ruined the restoration and comments ironically: **«¡Qué bonito!»** | Admiration versus ironic evaluation. A suitable ironic reading depends on shared context; B is not reliably obtained with slower speech, lower pitch, or a louder exclamation. Preserve *qué* stress and the phrase as a unit. |
| ES-X11 | A reader responds to an unexpected revelation: **«¡Vaya!»** | A clinician firmly tells a patient to go to the indicated room: **«¡Vaya!»** | Interjection versus formal imperative of *ir*. Same written word and punctuation; a bare-word surprise dictionary must abstain or report uncertainty. Do not invent a preceding gasp or extra word. |
| ES-X12 | A friend reacts to surprising news: **«¡No me digas!»** | A listener does not want a mystery's ending revealed: **«¡No me digas!»** | Conventional surprise formula versus literal prohibition. B can be urgent without being surprised; the text alone does not settle the intended act. Keep clitics attached in pronunciation and avoid splitting the short phrase. |
| ES-X13 | A frightened child is reassured after a harmless accident: **«No pasa nada.»** | An impatient adult dismisses a complaint to end the discussion: **«No pasa nada.»** | Reassurance versus dismissal. A can give space for the addressee; B can close the turn. Both can be quiet. Do not use the phrase as a guaranteed warmth, low-volume, or long-pause command. |
| ES-X14 | A speaker explicitly takes time to remember a name: **«Bueno…»** | A speaker accepts a proposal and hands the turn back: **«Bueno.»** | Hesitation/continuation versus completed acceptance. Respect written suspension, but do not synthesize extra filler words or infer that every *bueno* hesitates. An ellipsis can also indicate omission, so use the speech context. |
| ES-X15 | Ana is standing next to the speaker and is gently asked to approach: **«Ana, ven.»** | Ana is moving away into danger and is urgently called back: **«¡Ana, ven!»** | Addressed request versus urgent call. Keep the vocative distinct from the command while preserving the connection. A comma need not create a large silent gap, especially in B; loudness alone cannot reproduce a calling voice. |
| ES-X16 | After a question, the narrator identifies the speaker: **«¿Dónde está la llave? —preguntó Ana.»** Target: **«preguntó Ana»**. | A narrator contrasts who asked a question: **«No preguntó Luis; preguntó Ana.»** Target: **«preguntó Ana»**. | Background attribution versus contrastively focal narration. A can be less prominent and closely attached to the quotation; B highlights *Ana*. The verb *preguntó* must not make the tag itself sound like a question. |

For ES-X01–03, the lexical pronunciation of *espera* remains the same. Expression should not turn it into *esperá* or *esperó*. Voseo *esperá* is a different explicitly written form and requires the appropriate regional pronunciation; it is not an emotional variant produced by moving a stress marker.

## A useful expression lexicon contract

Keep three records conceptually separate:

1. **Pronunciation:** word identity, segmental reading, lexical stress, language and dialect. An exclamation mark is not permission to rewrite these.
2. **Evidence:** the exact matched span, punctuation, possible speech act, attribution/context support, rule identifier, and uncertainty. A whole short utterance match can be useful; a word found anywhere in a paragraph is insufficient.
3. **Realization:** a renderer's supported control, applied only if it has evidence and has been evaluated. A speech-act label alone does not entail a numerical pitch, rate, gain, or pause.

The narrow phrases *¡Espera!*, *¡Cuidado!*, *¿De verdad?*, and *No te preocupes* can supply candidate functions. They still admit quoted mention, fictional deception, sarcasm, or different addressees. Prioritize clear speech spans and abstain on unresolved ambiguity. ES-X11 and ES-X12 are explicit negative cases for unconditional surprise rules; ES-X13 is a negative case for guaranteed reassuring affect. *¡De verdad!* can be an assertion rather than surprise. *¿Qué bonito?* can be a repair question about what was said, and should not inherit an admiration label from *¡Qué bonito!*.

Coverage should count distinguishable supported functions and correctly rejected counterexamples, not the number of inflected strings in a pattern. Rule strength is not a calibrated probability of the speaker's intention. A high match score cannot substitute for context.

## What lightweight Kokoro control can do

The inspected local API accepts prepared phonemes, voice/style data, and a speed scalar. The narration layer separately represents terminal punctuation and speech-act evidence. It does not expose a validated Spanish Sp_ToBI-to-F0 controller. These are source-level observations as of this note, not acoustic validation. See [KokoroEngine](../Sources/KokoroKit/KokoroEngine.swift), [NarrationProsody](../Sources/KokoroNarration/NarrationExpression.swift), [expression evidence](../Sources/KokoroNarration/NarrationExpressionLexicon.swift), and [delivery](../Sources/KokoroNarration/NarrationDelivery.swift).

| Control | Useful scope | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Preserve final `?`, `!`, mixed marks, ellipsis, and internal punctuation | Retain textual cues already available to the model; avoid accidental statement endings after chunking | Punctuation does not specify information state, sarcasm, urgency, or a regional contour. A genuine continuation chunk must not receive the full sentence's terminal mark. |
| Keep a short utterance intact | Avoid separating *¡No me digas!* or a one-word directive into unrelated synthesis calls | Keeping it intact does not establish the intended speech act. |
| Plan phrase, speaker, and narrator-tag boundaries | Distinguish a local attribution from a new participant's turn; protect syntactic and discourse cohesion | A quoted speaker, narrator voice, and dialogue participant are different identities; voice-ID equality is not reliable turn-taking evidence. |
| Add bounded silence at known boundaries | Adjust total seam duration without changing words | Existing model silence contributes to the seam. A requested total is not an amount to add blindly. Silence cannot recreate overlap, an abrupt vocal cutoff, or a calling voice. |
| Expose a listener-selected rate and conduct controlled local rate experiments | Improve overall accessibility or test timing hypotheses with identical text/voice | Uniform rate scaling cannot independently position an accent, add selective syllable lengthening, or guarantee a question, whisper, shout, or interruption. No per-act multiplier is established by the papers above. |
| Keep uncertain expression as metadata | Enable later review or a future conditioned renderer while retaining a neutral fallback | Metadata does not itself improve the generated audio, and a rule-match unit test cannot establish an acting benefit. |

Do not append pitch arrows, introduce lexical tone tokens, move lexical stress, rewrite the words, or select arbitrary voice-pack rows to manufacture expression. In mixed-language narration, Mandarin tone-bearing symbols are linguistic content, not generic question/anger controls. A renderer would need independently supported pitch-accent alignment and duration control, appropriate training, or a validated style-conditioning mechanism to implement most of the detailed contrasts above. Postprocessing the whole waveform with pitch or gain shifts does not establish that capability.

## Timing implications for Spanish book dialogue

The following are engineering proposals for comparison, not measured native timing constants:

- Track *quotation → local speech attribution → resumed quotation* as a discourse structure. In **«—No —dijo Ana—. Todavía no.»**, both quoted pieces belong to Ana; the narrator's tag is not a new fictional participant's response.
- Distinguish a speech tag from action narration. **«—No. —Ana cerró la puerta.»** contains a new action sentence. Proximity to a quote alone should not receive the tag's shorter seam.
- Preserve **«—Espera… —murmuró Ana.»** as suspended speech plus attribution. Suspension and attribution are separate facts; neither should erase the other.
- A dash at a quotation edge may close a narrator insertion or delimit dialogue. Treat it as an interruption only when the parser/context supports an unfinished speech event. Do not convert all Spanish dialogue dashes into clipped delivery.
- For an explicit interruption, compare a short transition with the ordinary turn transition. Do not create overlap or trim nonzero speech solely because *espera* matched a dictionary rule.
- Use the total audible boundary, including existing trailing and leading silence, when evaluating seams. Low-energy fricatives and breath are not proven silence. Avoid accumulating sentence, speaker-change, paragraph, and expressive pauses for the same seam.
- Question marks alone do not prescribe an answer-sized pause: ES-X07's rhetorical continuation, ES-X08's choice, ES-X09's attached tag, and a narrator attribution project different continuations. Keep chosen constants configurable and name them as experimental targets.

## Evaluation and remaining uncertainty

Use the 16 pairs as context-conditioned review prompts, not an expected-F0 unit test. Keep text, selected voice, listener rate, pronunciation revision, rendering path, and playback gain matched. Review sentence function, lexical stress, word preservation, boundary continuity, and regional appropriateness separately. Ask listeners whether each reading fits its stated context and whether an alternative fits better; do not ask only whether it sounds “expressive.” Include the ambiguous and ironic cases even if a conservative renderer intentionally produces the same result for both.

Native speakers from the intended voice variety should judge those contexts. Peninsular, Mexican, Puerto Rican, and other Spanish varieties cannot be collapsed into a single expected curve; speaker style also matters. Useful acoustic diagnostics include accent alignment around the stressed syllable, voiced-region F0 movement, vowel duration, and measured seam duration, but they do not replace perceptual judgments. An isolated token or F0-tracking error is especially misleading for *¡Espera!*.

No audio was rendered or rated for this research note. No pause values, pitch shifts, expression-rule precision, Kokoro naturalness gains, or regional voice authenticity were validated. Several conclusions above rely on accessible author/publisher abstracts; full-text findings are identified where read. The practical near-term target is faithful wording, stress, punctuation, and discourse boundaries, with expression evidence that can abstain. More detailed acting remains a listening experiment or a model-conditioning project.
